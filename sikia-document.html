Case — Supreme Court

Uganda v. Kato Jonathan (Criminal Appeal No. 14 of 2021)

Supreme Court of Uganda Coram: Katureebe, CJ; Mwangusya, JSC; Tibatemwa-Ekirikubinza, JSC Decided 12 May 2021 Criminal Law · Evidence

Summary

The appellant, Jonathan Kato, was convicted by the High Court at Mukono of aggravated robbery contrary to sections 285 and 286(2) of the Penal Code Act. His appeal to the Court of Appeal was dismissed. He appealed to the Supreme Court on the ground that the identification evidence relied upon at trial was unreliable and that the learned trial judge failed to apply the well-known cautionary principles set out in Abdalla Nabulere & 2 Others v. Uganda [1979] HCB 77.

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal, set aside the conviction, and ordered the appellant's immediate release. The judgment reaffirms the duty of a trial court to exercise the greatest caution before convicting on the basis of identification evidence given under difficult conditions.

Facts

On the night of 3 April 2018, at approximately 9:45 p.m., a group of four assailants attacked the complainant at her home in Namataba village. The only source of light during the attack was a small kerosene lamp in the sitting room. The attack lasted between three and five minutes. The complainant later identified the appellant at an identification parade held eleven days after the incident.

The prosecution case rested almost entirely on the complainant's dock identification and her prior identification of the appellant at the parade. No independent corroborating evidence was adduced. The appellant denied any involvement and raised an alibi.

Issues

  1. Whether the trial court properly warned itself of the dangers of convicting on identification evidence alone.
  2. Whether the conditions of observation were sufficient to found a reliable identification.
  3. Whether the appellant's alibi was adequately considered.

Holding

Ratio decidendi

Where a conviction rests wholly on identification evidence given in difficult conditions, the trial court must expressly direct itself on the Nabulere cautionary warning, must test the quality of the identification against objective factors (distance, lighting, duration, impediments to view), and must look for corroboration. Failure to do so renders the conviction unsafe.

Applying that test to the present record, the Court found the trial judge made no express reference to the cautionary warning; the conditions of observation (poor lighting, brief duration, stressful circumstance) were plainly difficult; and the alibi was rejected without adequate reasons. The conviction could not stand.

“A mistaken witness can be a convincing one, and a number of such witnesses can all be mistaken. The trial judge must always remind himself of this before convicting solely on identification evidence.”
— Per Tibatemwa-Ekirikubinza, JSC, at para. 34

Discussion

The Nabulere caution

Since Nabulere, Ugandan courts have consistently recognised that honest witnesses can be mistaken. The Supreme Court in the present appeal reaffirmed that the cautionary direction is not a formulaic recital; it is a substantive self-direction that must be reflected in the trial judge's reasoning on the record.

Quality of observation

The Court revisited the non-exhaustive checklist used in Roria v. Republic [1967] EA 583 and endorsed its continued relevance: familiarity with the accused, duration of observation, lighting, distance, impediments to view, and the interval between observation and first description.

Treatment of the alibi

An accused who raises an alibi assumes no burden of proof. The prosecution must displace it. The Court held that the trial judge had reversed the burden by demanding corroboration of the alibi witnesses rather than testing whether the prosecution had disproved it.

Disposition

Appeal allowed. Conviction quashed. Sentence set aside. The appellant is to be released forthwith unless held on any other lawful warrant.
